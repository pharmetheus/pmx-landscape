2 Therapeutic Context

21. Analysis of Condition

The Applicant’s Position:

Acute myeloid leukemia (AML) is a rapidly progressing and heterogeneous hematopoietic
disease arising from genetic and epigenetic dysregulation in hematopoietic precursor cells
leading to the clonal proliferation of myeloid precursors with an inability to differentiate into
more mature cellular elements. As a result, there is an accumulation of these immature cells (ie,
leukemic blasts) in the bone marrow, peripheral blood, and occasionally in other tissues, with a
variable reduction in the production of normal red blood cells (RBCs), platelets, and mature
granulocytes. The increased production of malignant cells, along with a reduction in these
mature elements, results in a variety of systemic consequences. The clinical manifestations
include cytopenias, bleeding or bruising, fatigue, and an increased risk of infection, which may
all be accompanied by constitutional symptoms, metabolic abnormalities, and various other
complications.

Although AML is the most common form of acute leukemia in adults, it is a relatively rare
disease. AML accounts for nearly 2% of all adult cancer deaths in the United States (US). In
2024, 20,800 people were estimated to be diagnosed with AML, and 11,220 were expected to die
from AML (NCI, 2023). The median age at diagnosis is 69 years of age in adults, with
approximately 60% of patients diagnosed at >65 years of age and one-third of patients diagnosed
at >75 years of age. Overall, AML is a heterogenous disease in which high initial response rates
are observed followed by frequent relapse. More than half of patients who achieve complete
remission (CR) following induction therapy will eventually relapse within 1 to 3 years, and only
31.9% of patients with AML in the US will survive >5 years (Kumar, 2011; NCI, 2023). With
relapse, survival outcomes decline drastically; <10% of patients with relapsed or refractory (R/R)
AML will survive >3 years (Brandwein et al, 2020).
