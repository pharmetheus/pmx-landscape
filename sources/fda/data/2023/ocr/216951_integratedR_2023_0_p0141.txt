The toxicologic profile observed in studies in non-anemic monkeys up to 9-months in duration
was similar to that observed in long-term rat studies, with systemic thrombosis/hemorthage
linked to excess hematocrit (total hematocrits as high as 74% at the highest dose compared to
42% in controls) and platelet reductions observed at >10 mg/kg/day (28 times the clinical dose
based on body surface area). Findings also included dose-related gastric hemorrhage, erosions
and/or ulcerations at >10 mg/kg/day. At 50 mg/kg/day (40 times the clinical dose based on body
surface area), widespread and severe impacts of polycythemia occurred, which included subacute
inflammation, edema, and hemorrhage in the superficial brainstem and other evidence of
systemic congestion and bleeding, underscoring the importance of avoiding excess red blood cell
mass in patients. All findings trended towards reversibility after the recovery period. In the 9-
month monkey study, the NOAEL was determined to be 3 mg/kg/day (2.4 times the clinical dose
based on body surface area), based on the daprodustat-related toxic effects coincident with
excess red blood cell mass observed at >10 mg/kg/day (=8 times the clinical dose based on body
surface area). The narrow margin (3.3-fold dose-range) between the NOAEL and a significantly
toxic dose corroborate the narrow therapeutic index observed in the rat.

Upwards of 150 genes known to be involved in angiogenesis, glucose metabolism, cell
proliferation, and inhibition of apoptosis are regulated by HIFs in response to hypoxia (Selvaraju
et al. 2014). However, no toxicity studies were conducted in animal models of anemia, and
excess hematocrit in healthy animal models confounds identification of toxicity not related to
excess erythrocytosis and high blood viscosity. Indeed, risks associated with daprodustat
observed in long-term animal toxicology studies appear attributable to polycythemia and related
sequelae. While data in non-anemic animals may somewhat overpredict risks of the use of
daprodustat in patients with chronic anemia, the steepness of the dose-response curve for toxicity
and narrow therapeutic index observed in animals require caution during clinical use.

Reproductive risks of daprodustat were assessed in a comprehensive battery of studies covering
the entire reproductive cycle, including a fertility and early embryonic development study in
female rats, embryo-fetal development studies in rats and rabbits, and a pre- and post-natal
development study in rats. Adverse developmental effects in offspring observed in rats and
rabbits in the fertility and early embryonic development, embryo-fetal development and pre- and
post-natal development studies were considered primarily a consequence of maternal toxicity
related to excess red blood cell mass. No adverse effects on fertility were observed in male rats.
Impacts on fertility in female rats were consistent with the established consequences of maternal
toxicity during early pregnancy. These data are presented in Section 8.4, and the reproductive
and developmental toxicity findings are discussed in detail in Section 13.2.4. Labeling
recommendations are consistent with erythropoietin stimulating agents, as the data suggest risk is
related to daprodustat’s primary pharmacology (i.e., risks with hematocrit overshoot).

Daprodustat and its major metabolites were determined not to be genotoxic using in vitro and in
vivo assays, and daprodustat was deemed not to to pose a significant carcinogenic risk based on
a carcinogenicity assessment that included 2-year rat and mouse carcinogenicity studies.
