Approval

1. Background
Defencath is a catheter lock solution (CLS) which contains taurolidine (13.5 mg/mL)
and heparin (1,000 USP units/mL) as the active ingredients and citrate as a pH
buffer. Taurolidine is a derivative of aminosulfonamide-taurinamide. A proposed
mechanism of action for the antimicrobial activity is via transfer of methylol groups
that interact with bacterial and fungal cell walls causing damage to the cell walls and
inhibiting adherence of microorganisms to biological surfaces. Heparin is included in
the solution for its anticoagulant properties. Defencath is instilled within the lumens
of the hemodialysis (HD) catheter after HD treatment and withdrawn prior to the next
HD session. It is not intended for systemic administration.
The Applicant, CorMedix, originally submitted NDA 214520 on June 30, 2020, and
received a Complete Response (CR) letter on February 26, 2021, due to product
quality deficiencies. As for clinical data, the Applicant provided adequate information
to support the effectiveness of Defencath in reducing the incidence of CRBSIs in
patients with kidney failure receiving chronic HD through a CVC. Substantial
evidence of effectiveness is provided by data from a single, randomized, activecontrolled phase 3 clinical trial. In this trial, Defencath met the primary endpoint of
time to CRBSI in demonstrating a 71% risk reduction in CRBSIs (95% confidence
interval, 38%, 86%, p value 0.0006) in the Defencath arm versus the heparin
comparator arm. Please see the NDA Multidisciplinary Review and Evaluation

I. Safety Update
The Applicant has submitted results from a literature review in April 2023
evaluating adverse events related to the use of the product. Two papers were
found which included one case report in a patient receiving long-term parenteral
treatment and a retrospective study in adults receiving home parenteral
nutrition. In the retrospective study, the study drug was instilled and left in the
catheters after administration of parenteral nutrition. Upon re-access of the
catheters the study drug was flushed into the patients and not withdrawn. There
were mild to severe adverse events (AEs) related to the study drug with no
deaths. The events included severe AE’s such as dyspnea, flushing, rash, and
dizziness and more mild symptoms such as dysgeusia, nausea, and malaise. In
many of the patients, re-challenge of the study drug did not result in repeat AEs.
In the case report, the study drug was instilled into a chest tube catheter and
aspirated prior to administering treatment. The Sponsor concludes that these
adverse events will not alter the labeling of study drug and in these cases the
study drug was not used as intended. All reported AEs were also consistent with
the AEs seen in the clinical trial previously submitted by the Applicant.
In both the case report and retrospective study, the study drug was not used as
intended by the Sponsor. The study drug is only indicated for renal failure
patients who are receiving hemodialysis through a central venous catheter and
is meant to be withdrawn and not flushed into the patient. The use, catheter
type, and patient population in both studies are not consistent with intended
indication of the study drug and there is agreement with the Applicant’s
evaluation.

III. Limited Population Pathway for Antibacterial and Antifungal Drugs (LPAD)
Request
The Applicant again requested approval of the NDA under LPAD. The product
will be indicated to reduce the incidence of CRBSI in adult patients with kidney
failure receiving chronic hemodialysis through a central venous catheter as it has
only been evaluated in this specific population with unmet need. Seeking
approval under the LPAD pathway is appropriate as the safety and effectiveness
of Defencath CLS have not been established for use in populations other than
adult patients with kidney failure receiving chronic hemodialysis through a central
venous catheter, i.e., Defencath CLS has not been studied in other populations
with other types of catheters.

1. Executive Summary
1.1. Product Introduction
Defencath® is a catheter lock solution (CLS) which contains taurolidine (13.5 mg/mL) and
heparin (1,000 USP units/mL) as the active ingredients and citrate as a pH buffer. Taurolidine is
a derivative from aminosulfonamide-taurinamide. A proposed mechanism of action for the
antimicrobial actvity is via transfer of methylol groups that interact with bacterial and fungal
cell walls (Torres-Viera et al. 2000; Shah et al. 2002) causing damage to the cell walls and
inhibiting adherence of microorganisms to biological surfaces. Heparin is included in the
solution for its anticoagulant properties. Defencath is instilled within the lumens of the
hemodialysis (HD) catheter after HD treatment and withdrawn prior to the next HD session. It is
not intended for systemic administration. The product has Class III Drug-Device CE Mark
approval in Europe as a catheter lock solution since 2013 (https://www.cormedixeurope.com/files/cto layout/PDFs/E-NEU01-01-2015 IFU English.pdf).

1.2. Conclusions on the Substantial Evidence of Effectiveness
The Applicant has provided adequate information to support the effectiveness of Defencath in
reducing the incidence of catheter-related bloodstream infections (CRBSIs) in patients with
kidney failure receiving chronic HD through a central venous catheter (CVC). Substantial
evidence of effectiveness is provided by data from a single, randomized, active-controlled
Phase 3 clinical trial, LOCK-IT-100. In this trial, Defencath met the primary endpoint of time to
CRBSI in demonstrating a 71% risk reduction in CRBSIs (95% confidence interval, 38%, 86%, p
value 0.0006) based on the clinical adjudication committee (CAC)-assessed incidence of CRBSIs
in the Defencath arm versus the heparin comparator arm.
A single adequate and well-controlled trial was considered adequate to support the safety and
effectiveness of Defencath in reducing the incidence of CRBSIs in patients with kidney failure
receiving chronic HD through a CVC based on the following:
1. The persuasive and highly statistically significant findings on the primary endpoint of
time to CRBSI. CRBSI as defined in the trial is a clinically meaningful endpoint and
development of CRBSIs may result in significant morbidity and mortality, particularly in
this patient population who rely on their CVCs for life-saving HD.
At the interim analysis which was conducted when 28 CAC-adjudicated CRBSIs had
occurred, there were 6 CRBSI cases in the Defencath arm and 22 CRBSI cases in the
heparin arm with a statistically significant difference in favor of Defencath (p=0.0034). In
the final analysis conducted once all ongoing subjects completed the study, there were
9 CAC-adjudicated CRBSIs in the Defencath arm and 32 CAC-adjudicated CRBSIs in the
heparin arm (71% risk reduction, 95 % confidence interval, 38%, 86%, p=0.0006).

2. Therapeutic Context
2.1. Analysis of Condition
Catheter-related bloodstream infections (CRBSIs) are a significant public health problem,
resulting in mortality and significant morbidity, including increased hospital length of stay and
healthcare costs (Mermel 2000; Warren et al. 2006; Allon 2008; O'Grady et al. 2011). CRBSIs are
of particular concern in kidney failure patients who require vascular access for hemodialysis
(HD). Approximately 25% of U.S. HD patients use temporary or permanent central catheters as
their primary vascular access for dialysis (Tokars et al. 2000; Allon 2004). More recent data
suggest that up to 80% of patients still initiate HD with a tunneled HD catheter despite efforts
to promote the use of functional arteriovenous fistulas at the start of dialysis (Lok 2007; System
2017). Among HD patients, bloodstream infections occur 2.5 to 5.5 times per 1,000 catheterdays, translating to approximately 0.9 to 2.0 episodes per year (Allon 2004); thus making
prevention of CRBSI in the HD population an important challenge.
Strategies to reduce the risk of CRBSIs include routine infection control practices, such as use of
proper hand hygiene, aseptic technique, and appropriate skin preparation regimens (O'Grady et
al. 2011). While there are no FDA-approved prophylactic antimicrobial lock solutions, the
Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) guidelines for the prevention of intravascular
catheter-related infections recommend the use of prophylactic antimicrobial lock solutions in
patients with long-term catheters with a history of multiple CRBSIs despite adherence to best
practices (O'Grady et al. 2011).

2.2. Analysis of Current Treatment Options
There are currently no FDA-approved antimicrobial or antiseptic catheter lock solutions (CLSs)
for prophylactic use in the prevention of CRBSI. However, a number of antimicrobial and
antiseptic solutions are used off-label as CLSs to reduce the risk of CRBSI in susceptible patients
(Labriola and Pochet 2017; Golestaneh and Mokrzycki 2018). Antibacterial drugs which have
been used as CLSs for CRBSI prophylaxis alone or in combination include vancomycin,
gentamicin, ciprofloxacin, minocycline, amikacin, cefazolin, cefotaxime, and ceftazidime. Other
products, such as alcohol, taurolidine, and trisodium citrate, have also been used as CLSs
(O'Grady et al. 2011).

5. Nonclinical Pharmacology/Toxicology
5.1. Executive Summary
There are CLSs containing 1,000 units/mL of heparin approved for marketing in the United
States. A CLS containing taurolidine and heparin, similar to Defencath, is approved in the
European Union. Thus, there is significant human clinical experience with the components of
Defencath in CLSs.
In addition to its use in CLS, heparin has been approved for systemic use in the United States,
contributing to the clinical experience with this drug substance. Although taurolidine is not
present in any drug products or devices marketed in the United States, there are published
studies demonstrating that up to 5 g have been given intravenously over 0.5 to 2 hours to
human subjects without significant toxicity. The amount of taurolidine in 5 mL of Defencath is
0.0675 g, about 74 times less than the 5 g tolerated by acute intravenous administration. Thus,
if the maximum catheter fill volume of Defencath was accidentally pushed intravenously into
the circulation rather than pulled out of the catheter and discarded according to the labeling
instructions, it is unlikely to harm a patient. A rat study conducted with a diluted solution of
Defencath did not use high enough levels of the active ingredients to adequately explore the
toxicity of taurolidine and identify possible target organs of toxicity (if any). However, the
medical reviewers found that previous clinical experience with taurolidine (particularly as a
component of CLS) was adequate to support the clinical development of Defencath and the
toxicology reviewer deferred to their judgement. Thus, additional acute or repeat-dose general
toxicology testing in animals was not required for the components of Defencath.
Defencath is not intended for systemic administration, so the Division agreed to waive
reproduction toxicity studies for taurolidine and deemed that these data were not needed for
labeling.
Genotoxicity studies were required for taurolidine to label Defencath as a drug product because
it is considered a new molecular entity. Although taurolidine is positive for genotoxicity in vitro
regardless of metabolic activation (mouse lymphoma and Ames tests), it is negative in vivo.
Data from a study in rats demonstrated that micronuclei were not induced in bone marrow
polychromatic erythrocytes, and DNA damage was not detected in liver cells in a comet assay
when taurolidine was administered intravenously up to a maximum tolerated dose of
600 mg/kg/day for three days. This suggests that in vivo, taurolidine is quickly metabolized and
deactivated so that genotoxic substances related to this molecule are not able to reach DNA
and cause damage.
At the levels where taurolidine exposure could be achieved in the clinic during use of the
Defencath CLS, the risk of genotoxicity appears low.

Clinical Adjudication Committee (CAC)
The CAC was composed of five members, two board-certified in internal medicine and
nephrology, one board-certified in pediatrics and infectious diseases, one board-certified in
internal medicine and infectious diseases, and one board-certified in medical microbiology. The
CAC reviewed the clinical documentation and the blood culture results to determine if a
suspicious case was a CRBSI. The trigger for clinical suspicion included the signs and symptoms
as detailed in the section above PLUS a positive blood culture (one positive blood culture would
suffice for any organism with the exception of coagulase-negative Staphylococcus, which
required 2 positive cultures). Blood samples were obtained from a peripheral site (or either the
arterial or venous catheter hub or the arterial or venous dialysis blood line). Subjects had to
show signs and symptoms of CRBSI and no other apparent source of bloodstream infection.
The CAC was convened between the dates of December 2017 and October 2018 to review and
adjudicate potential CRBSI cases used for the primary endpoint analysis. The committee
evaluated the subjects for suspicious CRBSI events by reviewing the subjects’ medical records to
ensure that they met the clinical and microbiological criteria for a protocol-defined CRBSI event.
Statistical Analysis Plan
Analysis Sets
The primary efficacy analysis population was the full analysis population (FAP) including all
randomized subjects who received at least one dose of study medication. Subjects were
analyzed according to their allocated group determined by randomization. The safety
population included all patients who took at least one dose of study medication. For this
population, subjects were analyzed according to the treatment they actually received.
Control of Multiplicity

significance level of 0.0294 was used for both interim and final analyses. The fixed sequence
testing procedure was utilized in the efficacy analyses to control the overall type I error. The
comparison of the secondary endpoint of catheter loss for any reason between the treatment
groups was formally performed only if the analysis of the primary efficacy endpoint
demonstrated that Defencath significantly reduced CRBSI, and the analysis of another
secondary efficacy endpoint of loss of catheter patency was formally conducted only if the

Analysis of Primary Endpoint
For the primary efficacy endpoint of time to CAC-adjudicated CRBSI, if a subject had a CRBSI
adjudicated by the CAC, then time to CRBSI was the number of days between randomization
and the occurrence of a CAC-adjudicated CRBSI. Otherwise, a subject was censored at the
earliest of the following dates: date of first catheter removal, date of last study drug dose plus
three days, and date of study completion. Note that, according to the Applicant, three days was
added to the last study drug dose because the study drug would be withdrawn at the next
dialysis session, which would typically be about three days later.
A log-rank test was performed to evaluate the treatment difference. A Cox proportional hazard
model was utilized to obtain the hazard ratio of Defencath over heparin and its 95% CI for the
CAC-adjudicated CRBSI. The incidence rate of CAC-adjudicated CRBSI per 1,000 catheter-days
was calculated for each treatment group. The 95% CI for the incidence rate of the event per
1,000 catheter-days was also computed based on the assumption that the number of catheterdays followed an exponential distribution.

Analysis of Secondary Endpoints
The secondary efficacy endpoints were analyzed as time to catheter removal for any reason and
time to loss of catheter patency. Time to catheter removal for any reason was defined as the
number of days between randomization and first catheter removal if a subject had a catheter
removed; otherwise, a subject was censored at the study completion date or last study drug
dose date plus three days. Similarly, time to loss of catheter patency was defined as the number
of days between randomization and date of loss of catheter patency if a subject had an event;
otherwise, a subject was censored at the earliest of the following dates: date of first catheter
removal, date of last study drug dose plus three days, or date of study completion. The
secondary efficacy endpoints were analyzed using the same statistical approaches as those for
the primary endpoint.

treatment with a taurolidine-based lock solution may be related to whether the

contamination and if the source is extra-luminal, for example, secondary to an
exit-site infection from a gram-positive organism, the CLS may not be effective.

8.2.1. Safety Review Approach
The focus of the safety review is the single, Phase 3 trial, LOCK-IT-100. The secondary

Defencath or any of its components (taurolidine, heparin, or the citrate excipient) in ex-U.S.
postmarketing experience or clinical studies. These included gastrointestinal, hematologic,
metabolic, and neurologic adverse events reported in the literature.

Categorization of Adverse Events
Adverse events in LOCK-IT-100 were defined as any untoward medical occurrence that did not
necessarily have a causal relationship with the treatment. Collection of AE data began after a
subject had signed the informed consent and ended 28 days (+7 days) after removal of the last
dose of the investigational product. Treatment-emergent adverse events (TEAEs) were defined
as AEs that started or worsened after the subject’s first administration of investigational
product. A serious adverse event was an adverse event that was fatal, life-threatening, led to a
persistent or significant disability, congenital anomaly or birth defect, or required or prolonged
an inpatient hospitalization. AEs were coded with MedDRA Version 21.0 in LOCK-IT-100.

Since Defencath is a CLS, its potential impact on HD catheters including, biocompatibility and
catheter integrity were evaluated. Please refer to the Office of Pharmaceutical Quality (OPQ)

nonclinical testing for any potential impact of Defencath on the integrity of the HD catheters,
additional input was sought from the Center for Devices and Radiological Health (CDRH). Dr.
Qin Zhang and Mr. Richard Williams provided input from the perspectives of biocompatibility
and mechanical integrity, respectively. Please refer to their consult reviews in DARRTS, dated
January 26, 2021. The CDRH reviewers evaluated the Applicant’s data submitted for two test
HD catheter models (one silicone and one polyurethane): MC10143 (MedCOMP) and
CENFP19K/A (Merit).

mechanical integrity standpoint, Mr. Williams’ review noted that the bench testing done with
the two test catheters mentioned above was limited to the tensile test only. As testing was
limited to only two catheter types, both reviewers had raised concern about the lack of data
with other catheter types. Additional information was requested from the Applicant regarding
the testing performed.

8.3. Statistical Issues
There were no major statistical issues with the Phase 3 trial. A planned interim analysis was
performed when 28 CAC-adjudicated CRBSIs had occurred. The results demonstrated a
statistically significant treatment difference in the primary efficacy endpoint in favor of
Defencath, and therefore the study was stopped early using an acceptable method for
controlling the type I error. Additional subjects were randomized and treated between the
interim analysis and termination of the study. The final analysis based on all data through the
study completion yielded similar results to the interim analysis.
The primary endpoint was changed during the blinded study. The Division was notified of the
change and agreed. However, the treatment effect of Defencath appeared consistent across
various definitions of outcomes. Death is an important efficacy outcome. The additional
analyses where the deaths were considered as part of the event were performed, and the
results were consistent with the primary endpoint.

8.4. Conclusions and Recommendations
Defencath is being considered for approval under the LPAD pathway due to its potential to
reduce the incidence of a serious and life-threatening infection in a limited population of
patients, that is, kidney failure patients receiving HD through a CVC. Effectiveness in other
populations has not been demonstrated. A streamlined approach involving a single Phase 3 trial
was considered acceptable given the statistically persuasive results in the trial. For the purposes
of this NDA, the safety database of 398 subjects exposed to Defencath was considered
sufficient. The efficacy and safety review for this NDA focused on the Phase 3 trial.
Efficacy
The efficacy of Defencath was demonstrated in a single, randomized, controlled Phase 3 trial in
a population of patients with kidney failure receiving HD through a CVC. The interim analysis,
which was conducted when 28 CAC-adjudicated CRBSIs had occurred, showed 6 CRBSI cases in
the Defencath arm and 22 CRBSI cases in the heparin arm, with a statistically significant
difference in favor of Defencath (p=0.0034). The Data and Safety Monitoring Board
recommended early termination of the study based on the results of the interim analysis. In the

final analysis, Defencath met the primary endpoint of time to CRBSI with 9 cases of CACadjudicated CRBSIs in the Defencath arm and 32 in the heparin arm (p=0.0006) and an event

Safety
The safety of Defencath was supported by the single Phase 3 trial.
The safety finding of concern in LOCK-IT-100 was the difference in loss of catheter patency,
which occurred more frequently in the Defencath arm versus the heparin arm. Loss of catheter
patency is a serious concern for patients with kidney failure who may have limited vascular
access options. The mechanism by which taurolidine affects loss of catheter patency is not clear
but has been noted previously and was a specific safety endpoint for LOCK-IT-100 due to this
concern. The difference in rates of catheter removal between the Defencath arm and the
heparin arm however, do not appear to be secondary to loss of catheter patency. Although
there were differences in the rates of CRBSI between the two treatment arms, the main driver
of the overall observed difference in catheter removal rates between the two arms was the
greater number of CVC removals due to AV graft/fistula maturation in the Defencath arm.
Certain AEs, including HIT, hypersensitivity reactions, and hemorrhage/bleeding were noted
more frequently in the heparin arm than the Defencath arm; however, because heparin is a
component of Defencath, these AEs may potentially occur with Defencath administration and
were thus highlighted in this review, and will be communicated in the Defencath labeling.
The remainder of the AEs occurring in LOCK-IT-100 with a plausible relationship to Defencath
and its components were consistent with prior knowledge of the safety of taurolidine, heparin
and the citrate excipient and included hemodialysis catheter malfunction, thrombocytopenia,
nausea, vomiting, dizziness, musculoskeletal chest pain, hypocalcemia, and dysgeusia.
Overall, there were no major differences noted between the Defencath arm and the heparin
comparator arm with regard to the number of reported AEs, SAEs, and discontinuations. No
deaths in either treatment arm were attributed to the study drug.

Reviewer Comment: There is some evidence suggesting efficacy of taurolidine lock solutions based
on relatively small, open label, randomized trials from the literature. Studies 2, 3, 4 referenced in
Table 46 evaluated taurolidine lock solutions in non-HD populations. Study 1 was conducted in an
HD population similar to Defencath, however, it had several limitations including that the study
enrolled a small number of patients at a single-center and was unblinded. Additionally, the
formulations of the taurolidine CLSs used in these studies differed from the formulation of
Defencath, limiting generalizability of the study findings.
16.5.2. Observational Studies
The Applicant proposed using the results from two observational studies reported by Murray et al.
(2014) and Solomon et al. (2012) to support the efficacy of a combined taurolidine and heparin CLS
in reducing the risk of CRBSI.
The Murray study evaluated the effect of a heparin CLS versus a combined taurolidine and heparin
CLS on the rate of staphylococcal bacteremia. Data for 565 patients on chronic HD were obtained
from the UK National Health Service Greater Glasgow and Clyde and the National Health Service
Forth Valley Renal Unit Patient Record database. The information on incident staphylococcalpositive blood cultures was collected for 27 months between April 1, 2011 and June 30, 2013. The
first 15 months of observation evaluated the incidence of bacteremia while heparin was used as
the CLS. On July 1, 2012, a combined taurolidine and heparin CLS replaced heparin and the
incidence of bacteremia was assessed for an additional 12 months. The results demonstrated that
the bacteremia rate per 1,000 HD days was higher before the introduction of the combined
taurolidine and heparin CLS as compared with after its initiation of use (0.69 [95% CI: 0.51, 0.87]
versus 0.40 [95% CI: 0.25, 0.54], respectively).
The Solomon study assessed whether a combined taurolidine and heparin CLS reduced the
incidence of CRBSIs in patients with HD. All 106 patients in the study received the combined
taurolidine and heparin CLS at a clinical site in the UK. The incidence rate of CRBSI per 1,000 HD
days in these patients was then compared with 34 patients who received a heparin CLS in a prior
randomized clinical trial. The results showed that the rate in the patients who received the
combined taurolidine and heparin CLS was less than half of the rate observed in the patients who
received the heparin CLS (1.33 versus 3.25).
Reviewer’s comments: Both studies were observational and single-arm studies. Because neither
study contained a randomized control, we cannot be sure that other differences did not exist
between the test group and the control group that might be the reason for some or all of the
differences seen. Additionally, patient level data were not supplied so we were unable to conduct
additional analyses to explore the potential for bias.
