Embryo-Fetal Development Toxicity Study

A dedicated embryo-fetal development toxicity study was not conducted or recommended per
ICH S9 Nonclinical Evaluation for Anticancer Pharmaceuticals. A weight-of-evidence literature-
based assessment of the effects of PD-1/PD-L1 on embryo-fetal toxicity in animals, on normal
and abnormal pregnancy in women, and on immune-related response in transgenic mice and
patients has been performed.

Review of published literature and information on the well-recognized mechanism of action
revealed that blocking the PD-1/PD-L1 pathway resulted in fetal loss in animal models and that
PD-1/PD-L1 expression plays an indispensable role in maintaining a normal and successful
pregnancy. This literature-based assessment provides evidence of that tislelizumab may cause
fetal harm, increase rates of abortion or stillbirth and increase risk of developing immune-
mediated disorders or altering the normal immune response in fetuses if administered to a
pregnant woman.

Therefore, no embryo-fetal toxicity studies in animals were conducted for this risk assessment.

Prenatal and Postnatal Development Toxicity Study
No study was conducted or recommended per ICH S9 guideline Nonclinical Evaluation for
Anticancer Pharmaceuticals.

Based on the literature, PD-1 plays an important role in suppressing aberrant immune responses
by inhibiting the activation of autoreactive lymphocytes. It is reported that PD-1 and PD-L1
knockout mice develop a late-onset autoimmune phenotype, including lupus-like
glomerulonephritis, arthritis, and cardiomyopathy. A meta-analysis suggests PD-1
polymorphisms are associated with susceptibility to rheumatoid arthritis, ankylosing spondylitis
and type 1 diabetes. These studies suggest that inhibition of the PD-1/PD-L pathway during
pregnancy may cause or potentiate autoimmune diseases in infants.

The FDA’s Assessment:
The FDA generally agrees.

Studies in the literature demonstrate that the PD-1/PD-L1 pathway plays a critical role in
maintaining maternal immunological tolerance to the fetus during pregnancy.

PD-L1 is expressed in the human placenta throughout pregnancy (Petroff, et al. 2003).
Fetomaternal tolerance was studied in a mouse abortion-prone allogeneic pregnancy
model (CBA x C57BL/6) where PD-L1 expression was detected at the uteroplacental
interface of the placenta as early as 10.5 days post-conception (Guleria, et al. 2005).
