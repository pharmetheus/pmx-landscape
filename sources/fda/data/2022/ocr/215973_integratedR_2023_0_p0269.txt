7. Risk and Risk Management

7.1. Potential Risks or Safety Concerns Based on
Nonclinical Data

Nonclinical studies were conducted using both the oral and subcutaneous routes of exposure to
assess both systemic and local toxicity of lenacapavir. Both routes of exposure were used in
clinical trials; the oral as a lead-in and the subcutaneous route for maintenance given once every
6 months. Nonclinical safety studies with lenacapavir included good laboratory practice (GLP)
single and repeat-dose toxicology studies in rats and dogs; reproductive and developmental
toxicology studies in rats and rabbits; and in vitro and in vivo safety pharmacology and
genotoxicology studies. Thirteen-week and 2-year carcinogenicity studies in mice and rats,
respectively, are currently ongoing and will be part of a future postmarketing requirement
(PMR). Overall, the nonclinical safety assessment for lenacapavir was considered acceptable to
support marketplace approval from a pharmacology/toxicology perspective. Exposure multiples
mentioned in the summary text are based on total lenacapavir concentration. All pertinent studies
and findings are summarized in the following section. Full reviews for all studies are located in
Section II.13.2.

Safety Pharmacology and Pharmacokinetics

Please see the clinical virology review (Section III.19) for studies on mechanism of action and
antiviral activity.

In safety pharmacology studies, other than a slight decrease in respiration rate at 100 mg/kg,
lenacapavir had no adverse effects on other respiratory parameters, neurological functions or
changes in electrocardiogram parameters tested in rats and dogs.

The pharmacokinetic profile of lenacapavir (GS-6207) following IV or oral administration was
evaluated in portal vein cannulated male Cynomolgus monkeys and Beagle dogs with or without
the cytochrome P450 (CYP) inhibitor, aminobenzotriazole, to understand the role of CYP
metabolism of lenacapavir. The results from these studies indicated that pretreating dogs and
monkeys with aminobenzotriazole did not change lenacapavir oral exposure in dogs or monkeys,
but showed the role of intestinal metabolism of lenacapavir.

Lenacapavir was >98% protein-bound in all animals and humans (unbound fraction was 0.13,
0.83, 1.11, 1.45, and 1.46% in rats, dogs, cynomolgus monkey, Rhesus monkey, and human,
respectively) at a concentration of 10mM.

The highest tissue concentration of lenacapavir was detected in the liver up to 42 days followed
by kidney cortex, kidneys, pancreas, kidney medulla, brown fat, and thyroid in both intact and
bile duct-cannulated nonpigmented male Wister Hanover rats and intact pigmented Long Evans
rats. LEN was cleared from all organs by 28 days and from the liver by 56 days.
